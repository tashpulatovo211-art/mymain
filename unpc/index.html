How a tournament works

Teams of three or four high school students come on the day and are drawn into pairs, and both teams in a room get the same case.

A case is a short brief in Uzbek with one question about young people, language, culture or social habits. Teams get an hour to prepare, then each team presents for 7 minutes and answers the judges' questions for 5. In those 7 minutes a team should say what the problem is, what policy it proposes, who carries it out and why it would work, and the judges aim their questions at the weakest point, like the evidence behind a claim, the cost, or who would enforce it.

Then each team writes a short resolution and hands it in.

The judges pick the winning team in each room and the best speaker, who can come from the losing team.

The cases

These are the four cases from the first tournament. None of them needs any knowledge of tax, constitutional law or macroeconomics. The briefs are in Uzbek, and two of the questions below are our translations.

National identity and values among young people in a globalised world

How can Uzbekistan strengthen national identity and cultural values among young people without limiting their openness to global culture and international exchange?

The Uzbek language, a foreign language and a practical skill

How can an effective system be created so that young people, while keeping the Uzbek language and national identity, learn at least one foreign language and one practical profession or professional skill?

Translated from Uzbek.

Young people's responsibility for a healthy society

Identify the social behaviour or harmful habit that poses the greatest challenge to young people today, and propose a practical policy solution to address it.

Each team picks the habit it argues about.

A young person's own responsibility for national values

In an increasingly globalised world, what responsibility does an individual young person have in preserving national culture, language, traditions and historical memory? And how can the state encourage young people to preserve national values without forcing them to?

Teams answer both parts. The second part is translated from Uzbek.

How the judges score

The judges score each team out of 100 on six criteria, and the team with the higher total wins the room. If the totals are equal, the higher score for quality of the solution decides.

CriterionWhat the judges look forPoints
Understanding of the problemThe team names the real cause and who is affected, and it works from the case instead of general points.15
Quality of the solutionThe policy is specific and realistic for Uzbekistan, it says who does what, and it answers every part of the competition question.20
Argument and evidenceEach claim has a reason, and the team backs it with examples, data or cases from Uzbekistan or elsewhere.20
Answers to questionsThe team answers the question that was asked, says where its proposal is weak, and adjusts without losing its line.15
Communication and teamworkThe speaking is clear and within the time, every member takes part, and the team listens to the other side.15
Written resolutionThe resolution states the policy clearly, matches what the team presented, and includes any change the questions led to.15
Total100

The best speaker is picked separately, as the one student who argued and answered questions most clearly.

Judge a room

Judges score how well students think, argue, speak and write. They don't rule on whether a technical proposal is correct and they don't look up facts during the round, so you can judge from any field.

Reading the four cases beforehand is all the preparation you need. If you'd like to judge a round, message us on Telegram.
